An Atlas of Possible Careers humanities graduates at work
Prototype, placeholder data

Mapping career archetypes.

What do humanities graduates do? What are the main types of work? What are the common paths through the working world? This atlas maps fifteen kinds of working life, the institutions that turn out to be full of humanities graduates, and the routes between them.

1 the field

Fifteen kinds of working life

Each mark is a kind of work, not a job title. Color is the family it belongs to. Size is how much of a first decade is spent there.

Where a mark sits depends on what you ask the field to show. By default it is placed by expectation: family around the circle, and distance from the expected image of a humanities career by radius, so teaching and writing sit near the center while security work and portfolio practice sit out at the rim. That radius is an editorial judgment. The other two arrangements are computed, and they disagree with it in useful ways.

Switch between them under the field. Select any mark to open it.

2 word & image

The work everyone pictures

Writers, designers, editors, communications staff. This family is the one the stereotype is built from. It is real and well-populated, but far from the whole story. What is interesting about it is its shape rather than its size. More of this work is freelance, concurrent, and multi-employer than in any other family on the map.

What the real data would put here Named employer landmarks at archetype grain. These counts were unreachable at major-by-employer grain and are abundant once the majors are pooled.
3 care & public life

The public sector is a humanities employer

School districts, agencies, hospitals, congressional offices, resettlement nonprofits. Clinical and human services is reached mostly by way of a second credential rather than a second degree.

It is also where named institutions become legible: a district, a department, a committee office employs humanities graduates by the hundred, where a private firm employs one or two.

4 systems & ledger

Unexpected career paths are still well represented

Analysis, finance, technology, and the coordination work that feeds all three. These marks sit far out because they contradict the expectation, not because they are rare. The route to them is lateral, but it is not a shortcut: analysis, finance and technology are each held by more graduate-degree holders than any mark in word and image. The measured split is in what people studied, below.

Placeholder standing in for a real finding The credential ladder is measurable today and shown nowhere — project management, securities licensing, security certification, UX foundations, real estate, nursing.
5 enterprise & the room

Where the paths converge

Management, sales, founding, service are where a great many paths end up standing. A decade of experience commonly leads to responsibility for people and budgets.

One share is missing from the map on purpose. About a ninth of all person-years carry a role the taxonomy cannot read: generic titles, student and member roles, out-of-scope trades and uniformed service, and gaps. Those are excluded here rather than drawn as a sixteenth kind of work, and every share on this page is a share of the fifteen that could be read.

6 what people studied

The unexpected work is the credentialed work

Every mark on this map is fed by many backgrounds, and most are fed almost evenly. What separates them is not what people studied but how far they took it.

The marks placed furthest from the expected image of a humanities career are not reached by a shortcut. Analysis, technology and finance are held by more graduate-degree holders than writing, communications or creative work. The credential is doing the work the expectation assumed a different degree would have to do.

Open any mark to see what its arrivals studied, how far they took it, and which schools they came from.

What a graduate degree does to the path

Two degrees, then a kind of work. Everyone here took their bachelor's in the humanities or a humanistic social science and then went back for a second degree. The first column is what they studied first, the second is the field they returned for, and the third is where they were standing ten years into working life. What this is

A placeholder-data prototype for a qualitative companion to the Humanities Workforce portal. The portal answers how many. This asks what is worth knowing about, and is built on illustration rather than comparison.

What is invented here

  • Every count, share, and employer figure, except the education layer below.
  • All six people and their routes. Each is a composite, not a profile.
  • The radial placement, which is an editorial claim about expectation.
  • The movement and language arrangements are computed here, but from placeholder route weights and from this page's own descriptive text.
  • The description quotes.

The fifteen archetype names are real project vocabulary; the employer names are organizations that genuinely appear in the corpus, shown here with fabricated counts.

What is measured here

  • What arrivals studied, how far they took it, and the institutions they came from — on every mark plate.
  • The graduate-degree ranking in what people studied, and the shading it puts on the field.

These come from the 551,560-person archetype panel at career year 10 — person_year_archetype joined to education_person. The queries are recorded in docs/superpowers/specs/2026-08-03-atlas-education-provenance-design.md. Nothing else on this page is measured.
