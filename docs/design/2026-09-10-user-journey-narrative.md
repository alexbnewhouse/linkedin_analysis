Read it straight through and it should sound like someone describing the site to a colleague.
That is deliberate. If a step cannot be said plainly here, it is probably not clear enough to
build.

## How to edit this, so the chart can be rebuilt from it

Three things have to survive your edits. Everything else is yours to rewrite.

**One step, one heading.** Each numbered heading below becomes one box in the chart. Add a
heading and a box appears; delete one and it goes. Renaming a heading renames the box, so
rename freely.

**Say where the reader goes next.** Every step ends with a sentence naming what follows it.
That sentence is what draws the arrows. "From here they can only go on to the fan" is one
arrow. "They either narrow it down or keep looking" is two, and the words after either and
or become the arrow labels. If you add a step, say what leads into it and what leaves it.

**Keep the four kinds of step distinct.** A step is one of four things, and the chart colours
them: something the reader reads, a choice the reader makes, the moment the data runs thin,
or the reader leaving with something. Each heading says which in its first few words. If you
change a step's kind, say so in the prose and the colour follows.

There is a structural summary at the end. If it ever contradicts the prose, the prose is
right and the summary is stale.

## The journey

### 1. The reader arrives

Somebody arrives, usually on a phone, and usually not at a calm moment. The common case is a
student who has just been asked what they are going to do with their degree and did not have
an answer they believed. They are expecting a pitch, a quiz, or an email capture, and they
are holding a thumb near the back button.

What meets them is a single sentence about what this is, and immediately underneath it, a
plain statement of who is actually in the sample: two million public professional profiles,
486,000 of them held by someone with a humanities degree, 407,000 of whom can be followed
across their first fifteen years of work. The population statement is in the same breath as
the claim, not on a methods page.

The only decision here is whether to stay, and nothing is asked of them to make it. From here
they go on to the field.

The data is the corpus itself. What NHA writes is the opening claim and the population
statement, together, and it must not read as a mission statement.

### 2. They read the whole field

The first real screen shows every kind of work these graduates ended up in, all of it at
once, with nothing ranked and nothing drawn as the answer. This screen exists to settle one
question before any other: is there anything out there at all.

It matters that nothing is ranked. A ranked list invites the reader to look at the top item
and stop, and the top item is not the finding. The finding is the width.

From here they either narrow it down, or keep looking around, and looking around keeps them
on this same screen.

The data is the pooled year-ten destinations, which are computed and sitting in the portal
today. What NHA writes is the caption that makes an unranked picture feel like an answer
rather than a shrug, which is harder than it sounds.

### 3. They choose whether to narrow

This is the first fork, and it is a soft one. The reader can carry on browsing the field, in
which case they stay where they are, or they can decide to narrow it down, in which case they
go on to choose a starting point.

Nothing is lost by browsing, and the site never pushes. A reader who leaves at this point
should still have read one true sentence.

### 4. They choose where to start

Three ways in, and none of them is a quiz or returns a verdict. They can start from their
major. They can start from a description of work they would want to be doing, chosen from
eleven written in the first person. Or they can start from a job they are already curious
about, which is the door that makes the site useful to a senior rather than only to a
sophomore.

They can use more than one, and opening a second should feel normal rather than like starting
over.

The first two doors lead to the guess. The third door skips the guess entirely and goes
straight to who is already doing that work, because a reader who has already named a job does
not need to be asked to predict anything.

The data is fields of study, the eleven first-person descriptions, and the 13,933 job titles
held by ten or more people. What NHA writes is those eleven descriptions, in a student's
voice rather than ours. That is a writing job, not a data job, and it is one of the two or
three most important pieces of writing in the whole product.

### 5. They guess, and read that the guess was wrong

Before any number appears, the reader is asked what they think most people with this degree
ended up doing. One tap, and skipping is the same size as answering.

Then the answer: the guess was wrong, and so is nearly everyone's, because the largest single
kind of work is only about one in eight. There is no majority answer to get right. That is
the finding, not the reader's mistake, and the copy has to say so before it says anything
about the guess.

This is the one place where the site's whole argument lives in two sentences, and it is worth
spending real time on. Being right must not feel like winning either, or the device turns
into a game.

From here they go on to see where people who started here actually went.

There is no data behind this step. It is an interface device. What NHA writes is the prompt
and the reply, written to be encouraging rather than clever.

### 6. Where could I go

The first of the three real questions. Having set a starting point, the reader sees where
people who started in the same place actually were, ten years later.

It is a fan, not a ranking, and the caption is in the past tense and about people rather than
paths. Not "this pathway leads to" but "people who reached this work most often came from".

From here they go on to open one of those kinds of work.

The data is the origin-to-destination cells, all of which clear the reporting bar. What NHA
writes is the caption, in past tense.

### 7. Who is already doing it

The second real question, and the one a reader reaches by starting from a job rather than
from themselves. It shows what the people currently doing this work studied, and where they
began.

This is the same data as the fan, read backwards. Saying so in one line is worth doing,
because a reader who understands that the arrow runs both ways has understood the whole
instrument.

From here they also go on to open a kind of work, so the two questions converge on the same
next step.

The data is those same cells read in reverse, joined to education records. What NHA writes is
the one line explaining the direction rule.

### 8. They choose a kind of work to open

A short fork. Whichever of the two questions they came in on, the reader now picks something
that looks interesting and opens it. From here they go on to the route.

### 9. How would I get there

The third real question, and the one that turns a wish into something a person could act on.
Opening a kind of work shows the jobs people held on the way in, how long it took them, what
they studied, what certificates they carried, and who employed them.

The order matters more than the wording. What the work involves comes first, then how people
got in, then what they carried, then where it happened. Leading with how many people are in a
category tells a student nothing.

From here they can narrow to people who studied what they study, and they can also leave at
any point with what they already have.

The data is career steps at job-title grain, 4.3 million title-to-title moves, 1.67 million
certification records across 545,000 people, and employer and location on the steps
themselves. What NHA writes is five short panel introductions.

### 10. They choose to narrow to people like them

The reader asks to see only people who studied what they study. This is the right thing to
want and it is where the sample size falls off a cliff.

Either there are enough people, and they go on to the smaller group, or there are too few,
and they go on to the honest version.

### 11. They read the answer for a smaller group

The same answer, for a narrower group, with the number of people it rests on visible on the
screen rather than in a footnote. The count is part of the reading, not a caveat under it.

Most readers land here rather than in the thin case. Roughly 98.5% of people sit in a group
large enough to describe, which is the argument for building this view instead of fearing it.

From here they go on to leave with something.

The data is field of study crossed with start and destination. What NHA writes is the rule,
in one line: the number on the screen is the number this rests on.

### 12. Too few people to describe

Sometimes the reader's question lands on a group of fewer than ten people. Small groups are
counted and never named, because at that size we would be describing individuals rather than
a pattern.

The screen never goes blank and never blames the reader. It says plainly that this particular
combination is too small to describe honestly, gives the reason, shows the wider answer
instead, names exactly which condition was set aside to get it, and offers to put that
condition back and widen a different one.

The sentence that does the work is the one explaining that the shortfall is about protecting
individuals. Nobody feels rejected by a privacy rule. The grammatical subject of these
sentences is never the reader.

From here they too go on to leave with something.

The data is the cells below ten people, which are counted but never named. What NHA writes is
three sentences, one per rung of widening, and they need writing before the screen is built
rather than after.

### 13. They leave with something to do

The test of the whole site. A reader who leaves with a feeling has been entertained. A reader
who leaves with a certificate they could start now, a graduate step and who took it, a list
of employers in their state, and a page they can bring to an advisor has been helped.

At least one of those things carries a real date, and the date comes from the provider or the
programme, never from this sample.

From here they may come back.

The data is certifications, graduate enrolments, and employer and state, the last recorded for
about half the sample. What NHA writes is what each of those choices meant for the people who
made it, with the self-selection caveat given room rather than hidden.

### 14. They come back

Weeks or months later. The site says where they were last time and offers to pick it up, or
to start somewhere else. There is no login wall and nothing is stored about the reader.

The sentence has to read as recognition rather than tracking, which is a matter of wording
and of the assurance sitting right next to it.

### 15. Any noun becomes the next question

This is not a step so much as a property of every step, and the chart draws it as a loop.

Any job, employer, major or certificate named on the screen can be clicked and become the new
starting point. A reader who follows their curiosity three hops out never hits a dead end and
never has to press back.

This is what makes the site a place to explore rather than a report with tabs, and it is why
there is no results page anywhere in it. The loop runs from the route and from the smaller
group back to where could I go.

## The four things the site never does

These are not steps, but they govern every step, and they belong in any conversation about
the journey.

It never says what anyone was paid, because the corpus has no earnings. When a reader asks,
the site says so plainly, hands over the federal occupation code, points at the Bureau of
Labor Statistics, and returns to what it was showing.

It never ranks. No best majors, no top careers, no match score, no result telling somebody
what they are. A demand for a verdict gets the wide picture back.

It never describes fewer than ten people.

It never pretends to be everybody. This is people who keep a public professional profile,
which undercounts the ones who left the professional track, and that runs in the direction of
the story we want to tell. Which is exactly why it goes on the page rather than in a
footnote.

## The writing this asks for

Reading down what NHA writes: about two dozen short pieces, of which six carry the argument.

1. The opening claim and the population statement, together.
2. The eleven descriptions of kinds of work, in a student's voice.
3. The gloss on the guess.
4. The three widening sentences for when the data runs thin.
5. The line that makes a rare job read as real rather than as a warning.
6. What each parting choice meant for the people who made it.

None of these are captions. They are where the site either sounds like a person who respects
the reader or sounds like a dashboard, and no dataset decides which.
