# The research report: outline

**1 September 2026.** Companion to `2026-09-01-product-maps.md`, and deliberately a different
animal from it.

## What this is, and why it is not the website

The site and the report make the same case to two audiences who need opposite things.

| | The site | The report |
|---|---|---|
| Reader | an undergraduate, then their advisor | an advocate, a dean, a legislator, a journalist, a researcher |
| Question it answers | "is there anything out there for me, and can you show me something specific enough that I believe you" | "is this true, and how do you know" |
| Mode | descriptive, specific, explorable | comparative, aggregate, citable |
| Comparison | to the reader's expectation, and to nothing else | to external baselines, with the caveats stated in full |
| Test of success | the reader leaves with something to do | a hostile reader cannot take it apart |

**Comparison lives here and nowhere else.** That is the single rule that keeps the two
products from contaminating each other. The site never compares humanities graduates to other
graduates; the report does, carefully, in one clearly marked section, with the places our own
data refutes us stated before anyone else finds them.

## The extraction path, because the grant asks for two things

The Mellon revision names an analytic whitepaper *and* an advocacy briefing. Write one report
and extract the second from it rather than writing two.

```
  THE REPORT  (the methodological anchor, ~40-60pp)
        │
        ├──▶ THE BRIEF (≤4pp)  = summary + ch.3 + ch.4 + ch.7 + the one-page envelope
        │                        for the hearing, the trustee, the dean
        │                        nobody opens a link in a hearing
        │
        └──▶ THE SITE cites it; the portal's "brief me" button quotes from it
```

Everything in the brief is a subset of the report, never a rewrite, so the two can never
diverge.

# Outline

## Front matter — What we can and cannot tell you

**One page, before the executive summary, not in an appendix.** Every audience we consulted
said the same thing: show the bad news first and I will trust the good news.

Six bullets: no wages, ever · no unemployment · no satisfaction or wellbeing · no demographics
· no skills measurement · no causation. Then the sentence that governs the whole document:

> We can tell you where these graduates worked. We cannot tell you what they were paid, and we
> do not claim this is every graduate.

And the population statement, stated as a bias with a direction rather than a hedge: this is
people who maintain a public professional profile, K-12 teaching is one of the largest
humanities destinations and teachers are among the least likely to keep an active profile, so
our best story is probably undercounted and our error runs in the flattering direction nearly
everywhere else.

## Executive summary — five findings

One page. Each finding one paragraph, each with its number, its population, and its page
reference. Written last.

# Part I — The question

## 1. The case against the humanities, stated fairly

The argument in circulation is about earnings and employability, and it is made with
starting-salary tables. State it in its strongest form, from its own sources, without
strawmanning. A report that misrepresents the opposing case loses the reader who came in
skeptical, and that reader is the one we are writing for.

## 2. Why we do not answer it on its own terms

Three options were available and we take the third.

| | |
|---|---|
| Contest the earnings frame with proxies — seniority, employer prestige | **Rejected.** We have no wages. Proxy-for-pay is the critique we are least equipped to survive, and it concedes that pay is the right measure of a degree |
| Refuse the frame entirely | Honest, but risks answering a question nobody asked |
| **Pair our trajectory evidence with external earnings sources, by citation** | **Taken.** They supply the level of pay; we supply the shape of a career, which nothing else can |

**The firing-level rule:** we never blend our destinations data with an external wage table to
produce a number. We cite, we do not compute. A computed earnings estimate wearing a costume
gets taken apart line by line, and the people doing the taking-apart will be right.

## 3. What this dataset is

The corpus, the pipeline, the population, and the one thing this data does that no alternative
source does: longitudinal trajectory at role grain with education attached, at two million
people. Not ACS, not the Clearinghouse, not BLS, not a salary survey.

Coverage stated as a table, on its face, here rather than in the appendix:

| Axis | Coverage |
|---|---|
| Employment type | 100% |
| Seniority, fused | 95.2% |
| Education field, person-level | 87.0% |
| Industry sector | 53.0% |
| SOC occupation | 21.5% |

**The canonical population must be settled before this chapter can be written.** We currently
have five different groups all called "the humanities cohort," spanning a 25× range, and the
panel behind our movement findings is only 37.7% narrow-humanities.

# Part II — What the humanities workforce actually looks like

Each chapter states its claim, its evidence, the figure that carries it, and what it must not
be made to say.

## 4. There is no funnel

**Claim.** Nothing here is a pipeline. The largest single destination holds about one in
eight, so seven in eight are somewhere else, and employers are so dispersed that 93% of them
hired exactly one graduate in this sample.

**Evidence.** Destination distribution at year 10, effective destinations, top-bucket share,
employer dispersion. All computed and tested.

**Figure.** The distribution as a field, not a ranked bar chart. A ranked bar answers "what is
the most common job," which is the question we are trying to dislodge.

**Must not say.** That the space is wider than other majors'. Our own committed numbers put
every humanities cohort at or below the all-graduate baseline of 8.25 effective destinations
— English 7.01, History 8.04, Philosophy & Religion 7.86, Communication & Media 6.42, Arts
4.18. The comparative form is refutable from our own JSON. The descriptive form is
unassailable and says what we actually mean. **State this refutation ourselves, in this
chapter, rather than letting a reviewer find it.**

## 5. Six hundred thousand jobs

**Claim, and my candidate for the report's headline.** The canonical cohort held **638,368
distinct job titles**. 86.4% of those titles were held by exactly one person; singleton titles
account for **29.2% of all the jobs anybody in the cohort held**.

**Why this is the strongest thing we have.** It is a fact about the shape of the distribution
that no salary table can address, no anecdote can match, and no reviewer can reframe. It is
not comparative, so it cannot be refuted by a baseline. And it converts "you can do anything
with a humanities degree" from a slogan into a countable object.

**Figure.** The rank-size curve, with the ten-person reporting bar drawn on it so the reader
can see exactly how much of the distribution we are able to name (13,933 titles) and how much
we can only count.

**Must not say.** That this is unusual relative to other fields. We have not measured that,
and long tails are a general property of job-title data. The claim is about magnitude and
about what it means for a student, not about superiority.

## 6. The first decade has two modes

**Claim.** Three in five people changed the kind of work they do over their first ten years.
Two in five did not. Both are ordinary.

**Evidence.** Among 238,967 people observed for all ten years: 41.7% held one kind of work
throughout, 35.1% held two, 17.2% three, 6.0% four or more. Mean 1.89. Flat seniority 24.8%.
One employment type throughout 89.2%. By field, from Theology at 51.7% and Fine & Performing
Arts at 51.4% down to Humanistic Social Science at 36.0%.

**This is an honest negative and it ships.** We had planned to lead with "almost nobody's
first decade is a straight line." Measured, that is false at this grain, and publishing it
would have handed a reviewer a free win. Report the distribution instead. It is a better
finding: it says that both stability and movement are normal outcomes of a humanities degree,
which is a more useful thing to tell a nervous parent than a slogan about churn.

**Caveat that goes in the text, not a footnote.** The panel keeps one role per person per
year and drops concurrent work — 2,524,265 secondary steps, 23.4% of all steps. Every movement
number here is a lower bound and portfolio careers are structurally invisible.

## 7. How people got where they are

**Claim.** People who reached a given kind of work most often came from these places, studied
these things, and carried these credentials.

**Evidence.** The origin→destination matrix at career years 1 and 10: 144 cells, all clearing
the ten-person bar, 267,001 people with both endpoints, median cell 614. Plus educational
provenance and the graduate step.

**The direction matters and is not a stylistic choice.** Backwards from the destination is the
only direction this data supports. Forwards is a prediction and we cannot make one. Every
sentence in this chapter reads "people who reached X came from Y," never "Y leads to X."

**The graduate step deserves its own section.** Measured on start-dated enrolment within five
years of the anchor: History 39.2%, English & Literature 35.1%, Philosophy & Religion 34.1%,
Communication & Media 16.9%, Fine & Performing Arts 16.3%. Written as a claim about "the
humanities" it is wrong about most of our windowed people. Written per field it is strong,
specific, and useful.

## 8. Concrete places

**Claim.** Here is where these graduates actually worked, and here is how dispersed that was.

**Evidence.** Employer × occupation cells on the career-entry axis — 1,716 clear the bar,
against 41 on the graduation axis. Geography: 51.6% of steps carry a US state unconditionally,
but 94.9% of resolved US locations do.

**The honesty is the finding, not the caveat.** The largest first-five-year employers are
self-employment, the US military, and large retail. The legible cells sit underneath them.
**The moment we reorder that list to lead with the interesting employers we have stopped being
descriptive.** Show the whole shape: the big employers are big because they are big, and the
interesting cells are underneath. Ship the cell count, the suppressed-cell count, and the
singleton share with any named list.

## 9. What kind of working life

**Claim.** Independent practice grows across the decade and frontline service shrinks by more
than half — and employment type is the only thing we know about everybody.

**Evidence.** Founders and independent practitioners 2.4% of person-years at year 1 → 3.8% at
year 10. Frontline service 4.0% → 1.5%. Coverage 100%.

**Why it belongs in the report.** Two of our three audience personas independently called
employment type our most underrated asset, and the frontline decline is the cleanest
resilience evidence we have: people move up and out. It is also the finding least vulnerable
to a coverage attack.

# Part III — Where our argument is weak

**This part exists because the report is not an advertisement.** An advocate who has read it
should be able to anticipate every attack, and a reviewer should find that we got there first.

## 10. The Arts exception

Fine & Performing Arts: 4.18 effective destinations against a baseline of 8.25, a top bucket
of 24.2%, 51.4% holding one kind of work for the whole decade, and the lowest graduate-study
rate alongside Communication & Media. **Arts is the most pipeline-like group in our set.** Any
sentence about "the humanities" as a bloc is wrong about them, and they are 7,519 windowed
people. Say so in its own section rather than letting the exception surface as a gotcha.

## 11. Three things we cannot measure

**Skills.** LinkedIn's skills section was never captured. There is no skills field. Every
proxy — a title, a self-written description, a certification — is collinear with the move it
is supposed to explain. NHA's account of what humanities coursework builds is a claim NHA is
entitled to make; it appears in this report as NHA's claim, typographically distinct from
every measured number, and never as a finding.

**Coursework.** The courses table covers 5.6% of humanities people, carries no date,
institution, or link to a degree, and the most-listed courses among humanities majors are
Project Management, Public Speaking, Typography, Marketing, Statistics and Financial
Accounting. It cannot carry the claim. Educational provenance replaces it, and answers the
better question anyway: not "what will I learn" but "what did the people who got there study."

**Causation.** Everything here is a descriptive association among self-selected people. The
choices chapter — double major, graduate school, internship, military service, service year,
self-employment — reports what happened to people who made each choice and never what the
choice caused.

## 12. Comparisons we decline to make

Four, each with the reason, because declining to make a claim is itself evidence of
seriousness.

1. **Breadth against other majors.** Refuted by our own numbers (ch. 4).
2. **Convergence into leadership.** Humanities sit below the all-graduate baseline in
   management at every career age and the gap widens with age. And 28% of humanities
   management person-years at year 1, rising to 40% by year 15, carry a code that collapses
   founder, owner and "Head" into Chief Executives. The convergence result also only holds in
   our internal taxonomy and not on the public one — a reviewer will say we chose the taxonomy
   that gave the answer, and they will be right. **What survives, and it is worth more:
   responsibility accumulates within the humanities population, and administration is a real
   door into it.**
3. **Bigger career leaps than technical backgrounds.** Requires a skills measurement we do not
   have, on populations we cannot compare fairly.
4. **Any earnings or ROI claim.** Not a gap. A permanent boundary.

We also do not report downward mobility. Our downward-move signal agrees with explicit title
evidence about half the time, which is a coin flip, and we do not sell coin flips.

# Part IV — What follows

## 13. For students and their advisors

What the evidence supports saying in an advising conversation, and what it does not. Points at
the site and the portal as the operational form of this chapter.

## 14. For departments and institutions

The provenance findings, the graduate-step findings by field, and the institution-type facet.
The framing an institutional audience needs: this is a destination and durability instrument,
not an ROI instrument, and a percentage here is never a percentage of *your* graduates.

## 15. For the field

What we would need to answer the questions we could not: the occupation-coding gap, the
concurrency data nobody has read, wage linkage by partnership rather than by inference, and a
refresh cadence. Written as an invitation to collaborate rather than a list of regrets.
