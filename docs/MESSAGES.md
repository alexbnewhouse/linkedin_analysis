# The eight messages: what we mean, how we measure them, where they go

**Draft for discussion. 24 August 2026.**

One page per message. Each has the same five parts:

- **What we mean by it.** A proposed working definition, so we are all arguing about the
  same claim.
- **How we measure it.** What in the data stands behind the message.
- **Where it goes: the research report.** How the message is made to an advocate, a provost,
  a journalist, or a researcher.
- **Where it goes: the website.** How the same message is made to an undergraduate or their
  advisor.
- **What is still needed.**

**A note on the two products, because it governs every page below.** The report and the
website are making the same eight arguments to two audiences who need opposite things. The
report is comparative, aggregate, and built to be cited and quoted. It answers "is this
true, and how do you know." The website is descriptive, specific, and built to be explored.
It answers "is there anything out there for me, and can you show me something concrete
enough that I believe you." The same finding usually takes a different form in each. Where
that difference matters, it is spelled out.

**Status vocabulary used throughout:**

| | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Ready** | Measured, checked, and available today |
| **Measured, not yet published** | The analysis is finished. Nothing currently shows it |
| **Needs new analysis** | Real analytical work still to do |
| **Needs writing** | A writing task. No new analysis required |

## Message 1. A humanities degree opens a possibility space, not a single pipeline

**Status: Ready.** This is our strongest and best-evidenced message.

### What we mean by it

A humanities degree does not funnel graduates toward one job or one industry. The people who
hold these degrees are spread across a very wide range of work, and no single destination
accounts for more than a small minority of them. The right mental picture is a field of
possibilities, not a track.

Note what we are not claiming: not that the space is wider than for other majors, and not
that any particular destination is likely. The claim is about the absence of a funnel, and
that is both truer and more useful than a comparison.

### How we measure it

Three measurements, all complete:

- **Concentration.** We look at where people are ten years into their careers and ask how
  much of the group the most common destination accounts for. Across our five fields the
  answer runs between 12 and 15 percent. Roughly seven in eight people are somewhere else.
- **Range.** We count how many distinct kinds of work a field's graduates reach. English
  graduates alone appear in 16 of the 23 major occupational categories.
- **Employer spread.** Across all the employers who hired graduates in this data, 93 percent
  hired exactly one. The dispersion is extreme, and it is measurable.

### Where it goes: the research report

This is the report's opening finding and the frame for everything after it. It is presented
as a distribution with the concentration figures given per field, because an advocate needs
a number they can say out loud and defend. The employer dispersion figure is the most
striking single statistic we have and should be quoted directly.

### Where it goes: the website

This is the site's first screen and its thesis. It shows the whole spread of destinations at
once, as a field rather than a ranked list. That form choice carries the argument: a ranked
bar chart answers "what is the most common job," which is exactly the question we are trying
to dislodge. One plain sentence sits under it, something close to: the largest single
destination holds about one in eight people.

The student version leads with the picture. The report version leads with the number.

### What is still needed

Nothing analytically. The screen exists and works; it needs a design pass to make it more
confident, and the report section needs writing.

## Message 2. Careers unfold over time

**Status: Needs new analysis (small).**

### What we mean by it

A career is not a destination reached at graduation. It is a sequence that develops over
years, and the open-endedness of a humanities career is the normal case rather than a sign
that something has gone wrong. Straight-line careers are rare for almost everyone.

### How we measure it

We can follow the same people year by year across their first ten to fifteen years and see
where they were at each point. Two things this gives us:

- **The picture at intervals.** Where a group stood at year one, year three, year five, and
  year ten, and how the distribution changes between them. This is measured and available.
- **How much movement there is.** More than half of job changes move a person into a
  different kind of work, and around a third move them into a different industry. Over the
  first ten years, most people hold more than one kind of work.

**The number we do not yet have, and should.** How rare the straight line actually is. The
share of people whose first decade is one occupation, or one employer, or a flat trajectory.
This is a modest piece of analysis and it would convert "careers unfold over time" from a
sentiment into a finding. It is the single most valuable uncomputed number we have.

### Where it goes: the research report

As the headline that makes non-linearity normal rather than exceptional. This is the message
that most directly serves the advocacy goal of getting an audience to stop treating a
humanities graduate's winding path as evidence of a problem. Presented as the linearity
figures plus the movement rates.

### Where it goes: the website

As a screen a student moves through, watching the group redistribute across the decade. The
strongest device available here is asking the student what they expect before showing them
what happened. That turns a chart into a small moment of surprise, and it is well matched to
findings that invert expectations.

The report states that careers are non-linear. The website lets a student watch it happen and
then tells them it is normal.

### What is still needed

- The linearity analysis described above.
- Rebuild of the underlying year-by-year dataset, which is currently out of date. This is
  routine but it needs to happen before any of these numbers are quoted.

## Message 3. Humanities careers frequently reach unexpected destinations

**Status: Ready.**

### What we mean by it

The destinations most people assume for a humanities graduate are taken by a minority of
them, and the range of places they actually end up includes many that would surprise a
parent, a legislator, or the student themselves.

"Unexpected" here means unexpected relative to the stereotype. That is the comparison worth
making, and it is a comparison to an assumption rather than to another group of graduates.

### How we measure it

Two ways:

- **Concentration in surprising places.** Some fields land in particular kinds of work far
  more often than graduates overall do. History graduates enter legal work at about four
  times the all-graduate rate. Philosophy and religion graduates enter community and social
  service work at nearly nine times. Arts graduates enter design and media at about seven
  times. These are strong, clean signals.
- **The assumed destination is a minority.** For every field, the destination people picture
  accounts for a modest share of graduates. That is the other half of the message and it
  comes from the same measurement as Message 1.

### Where it goes: the research report

As a table of the distinctive destinations per field. This is the material an advocate uses
to answer "what do history majors even do," and the concentration figures are quotable.

### Where it goes: the website

As the layer that opens up. Each broad destination on the site can be opened to show the
specific job titles inside it, which is where a student stops seeing a category and starts
seeing a job. This already works.

It also belongs in a set of short myth-correction pieces, each one taking an assumption and
answering it with a measured number.

The report names the surprising destinations. The website lets a student open one and see
what the work actually is.

### What is still needed

Editorial work rather than analysis: deciding which surprises to lead with per field, and
writing the myth pieces. The measurements exist.

## Message 4. Transferable skills enable career moves

**Status: Ready in part. Needs new analysis for the credential half.**

### What we mean by it

The kind of work a humanities graduate does is not fixed by their major, and it is not fixed
by their first job. People move between kinds of work and between industries repeatedly, and
the capabilities a humanities education builds are what make those moves possible.

An important distinction that should be visible in how we present this. We can measure the
moves directly. The skills themselves are NHA's account of what a humanities education
builds, drawn from the Career Skill Archetypes framework. Both belong in the message; they
should be visibly different kinds of claim, with the skills language presented as NHA's
position rather than as a measurement.

### How we measure it

- **The moves.** More than half of job changes put a person into a different kind of work.
  About a third put them into a different industry. Most people hold more than one kind of
  work across their first ten years. This is the measured backbone of the message.
- **The skills language.** The framework already articulates these in coursework terms:
  close reading, rhetorical analysis, source evaluation, archival research. This is ready to
  use and needs no analysis.
- **Credentials people add.** Our data records professional certifications, and they show
  humanities graduates converting a degree into specific occupational entry. Project
  management, notary, securities licensing, IT security, real estate, Scrum, user
  experience, nursing. This is the most concrete and actionable material in the entire
  dataset and nothing currently uses it.

### Where it goes: the research report

As the movement rates, presented as evidence of adaptability. The report's version is
careful about the distinction above: it reports the moves as measurement and the skills
account as NHA's framework, and it is stronger for being explicit about which is which.

### Where it goes: the website

This is where the message becomes useful rather than reassuring. Three layers:

1. What your coursework builds, in the framework's language.
2. Where that shows up in the working world.
3. What people actually did next, including the specific certifications they added.

The third layer is the one a student can act on this semester, and it is the only thing in
the whole project that converts into a next step.

The report argues that humanities graduates adapt. The website tells a student what adapting
concretely looked like for the people who did it.

### What is still needed

- Build the certifications analysis. The underlying records exist and have never been used.
- Write the skills layer from the framework.

## Message 5. You can chart a course to a destination, and be prepared for what arrives

**Status: Measured, not yet published.**

### What we mean by it

Aiming at something is worthwhile, and it does not close off the alternatives. People who
set out toward one kind of work frequently arrive somewhere related and unforeseen, and the
preparation that got them moving is what let them take the opportunity when it appeared.

### How we measure it

The honest and useful version of this runs backwards. For any destination, we can show where
the people who reached it came from. That produces statements like "the people doing this
work arrived from these five kinds of starting point, in these proportions" — which is
descriptive, checkable, and far more interesting than a forecast.

The complementary measure is spread from a common origin: people who started in the same
kind of work were, ten years later, in a wide range of different places. Both come from the
same year-by-year movement data, which is built and currently displayed nowhere.

### Where it goes: the research report

As evidence that entry points are not commitments. This is a supporting finding in the
report rather than a headline, and it works best next to Message 2.

### Where it goes: the website

This is a genuinely useful screen and it is the one most directly aimed at reassurance. A
student picks a destination that interests them and sees the range of places people came
from to get there. Seeing five different origins converge on one destination does more for
confidence than any amount of encouragement.

The framing rule matters here and should hold on both products: we always describe where
people came from, never predict where someone will end up.

The report treats this as evidence. The website treats it as reassurance.

### What is still needed

Connect the existing movement data to a page. The analysis is done; there is no surface for
it.

## Message 6. Many career paths converge in leadership positions

**Status: Measured, not yet published.**

### What we mean by it

Across many different starting points, a substantial number of humanities graduates are in
positions of responsibility and organizational leadership by mid-career. The routes there
are varied, and several of them run through roles that do not look like leadership at the
outset.

### How we measure it

- **Responsibility accumulates.** We track a measure of seniority across the years of a
  career, and it rises steadily across the first decade and beyond.
- **The routes converge.** We can trace three-stage routes across years one, five, and ten,
  and a striking number end in management from very different beginnings. Coordination and
  administration into management. Sales into management. Analysis into management. Policy
  and research into management. Teaching into management. Creative work into management.
  Each of these is a route travelled by roughly a thousand people or more in our data.
- **The administrative entry route.** About one in ten of all the years we observe are spent
  in administrative and coordination roles. Those roles are widely read as a dead end. In the
  data they are one of the most common on-ramps to organizational leadership, and that is a
  genuinely encouraging finding we are not currently telling anyone.

### Where it goes: the research report

As the accumulation finding: a decade adds responsibility, and it does so from many
different starting points. Presented within the humanities population rather than as a
ranking against other graduates.

### Where it goes: the website

The administrative on-ramp is the piece to lead with, and it should be phrased for a student
who has just been offered a coordinator job and is worried it is a detour. Showing that this
is one of the best-travelled routes into leadership is, in plain terms, good news that
happens to be true.

The report makes the structural point about accumulation. The website makes the personal
point to someone about to take an entry-level job.

### What is still needed

Connect the route data to a page. It is measured and unpublished.

One decision to record: this message should not carry any claim about earnings. We have no
pay data of any kind, and any earnings statement would have to be sourced from elsewhere and
cited rather than computed by us.

## Message 7. Your coursework gives you skills that translate to careers

**Status: Measured, not yet published. Needs new analysis to correct the population.**

### What we mean by it

What a person studies connects to the work they go on to do, and that connection is much
broader and less literal than "study X, become an X." The strongest form of this message
runs from the destination backwards: the people doing this work studied these things, to this
level, at these kinds of institutions.

This is a deliberate reframing of the original message and I think it is a stronger version
of it. It answers the question a student is actually asking, which is not "what will I learn"
but "what did the people who got there study."

### How we measure it

- **Educational provenance.** For any kind of work, we can show the fields the people doing
  it studied, how far they took their education, and what kinds of institutions they attended.
  The patterns are sharp and interesting. People in education roles are overwhelmingly
  graduate-degreed. People in creative work are mostly bachelor's-terminal, and predominantly
  studied fine and performing arts. Legal and policy work is heavily graduate-degreed.
- **The graduate-school step.** This is one of our best-evidenced findings and it currently
  sits in a single panel. Within five years of finishing, 39 percent of history graduates and
  35 percent of English graduates enroll in a graduate or professional degree. History
  graduates go to law school at about five times the all-graduate rate. Arts graduates pursue
  an MFA at about nine times. The figure is much lower for arts and for communication and
  media, at roughly one in six, so this is a per-field message rather than a blanket one.
- **Undergraduate to graduate combinations.** We can trace 40,000 people from a humanities
  bachelor's through a graduate degree in a named field and on to their work, across fifteen
  graduate field families including business, education, law, public administration, and the
  health professions.

### Where it goes: the research report

The graduate-study finding is the report's strongest single piece of evidence on this
message and deserves a section of its own. It is precise, per-field, and directly answers
the question institutions ask about what a humanities BA prepares a student for.

### Where it goes: the website

The graduate step should be its own screen rather than a panel inside something else. It
works as a three-sided view: what someone studied as an undergraduate, what they studied
next, and what work they went into, enterable from any of the three.

Alongside it, each kind of work on the site carries a "what they studied" panel, so a
student looking at a job can see the educational routes that led there.

The report gives the per-field enrollment rates. The website lets a student trace their own
field through to a destination.

### What is still needed

- Recompute the educational-background figures on a corrected population. The current version
  is calculated over a broader group than intended, which changes the numbers.
- Promote the graduate-school view out of the prototype and into its own screen.

## Message 8. These are concrete career pathways

**Status: Needs a decision, then new analysis.**

### What we mean by it

We can name real places where real graduates worked, real job titles they held, and real
sequences they moved through. Not illustrative examples and not composites. Specific,
checkable facts about where people with these degrees went.

This is the message that separates our project from every general argument about the value of
the humanities, and it is the one our audiences want most.

### How we measure it

We can report an employer together with the kind of work done there, wherever at least ten
graduates in our data are in that cell. On the measurement approach we recommend, that yields
around 1,700 nameable employer-and-occupation combinations, plus around 1,500 that can also
carry a state. Named examples that clear the bar include the U.S. House of Representatives,
New York University, New York City public schools, and Condé Nast.

**One decision gates all of this, and it needs to be made deliberately.** We can measure a
person's career either from the year they graduated or from the year of their first job.
Graduating is the more natural reference point, but we only know the graduation year for
about one graduate in five, which shrinks what we can name from roughly 1,700 combinations
down to about 40. Measuring from first job keeps almost everyone. The cost is that we can say
"in the first five years of working life" but not "five years after graduating."

The recommendation is to measure from first job and keep the graduation-based version as a
smaller cross-check.

### Where it goes: the research report

As the evidence that the argument is grounded. The report presents the named employers along
with the spread: how many employers there are in total, and how many hired only one graduate.
The spread is not a footnote to the list, it is the point. It is what shows that these are
real destinations rather than a funnel.

### Where it goes: the website

This is the screen students will use most. Employers, the kind of work done there, and where
in the country. It is also the screen where the honesty and the usefulness pull hardest
against each other, because the biggest employers are large national ones while the
interesting ones are smaller and more specific. Both need to be visible at once.

Geography belongs here too. "Where could I work if I stay near home" is among the most
concrete questions a student has, and we have never answered it.

The report uses named employers to prove the argument is real. The website uses them to give a
student somewhere specific to look.

### What is still needed

- The measurement decision above. Everything else waits on it.
- Build the employer, occupation, and geography view.
- A review of how much geographic detail we can support before designing around it.
